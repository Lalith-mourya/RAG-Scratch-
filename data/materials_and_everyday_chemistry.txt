MATERIALS, CHEMISTRY, AND EVERYDAY OBJECTS

Materials are selected for their physical, chemical, and mechanical properties. Metals often conduct heat and electricity well and can be shaped into useful forms. Ceramics are commonly hard and heat-resistant but may be brittle. Polymers consist of long molecular chains and can range from flexible films to rigid structural materials. Composites combine two or more materials to obtain a combination of properties not easily achieved by one material alone.

Atoms contain a nucleus surrounded by electrons. The number of protons in the nucleus determines an element's atomic number. Atoms can bond through interactions involving their electrons. In ionic bonding, electrons are transferred or redistributed so that oppositely charged ions attract. In covalent bonding, atoms share electron pairs. Metallic bonding involves metal atoms whose outer electrons are relatively mobile, helping explain many metals' electrical conductivity and ability to deform.

Chemical reactions rearrange atoms and change how they are bonded. The total number of atoms of each element is conserved in an ordinary chemical reaction, even though substances and their properties change. A balanced chemical equation represents this conservation. Reaction rates can depend on temperature, concentration, surface area, catalysts, and the nature of the reactants.

Acids and bases are described by several related chemical models. In aqueous solution, pH is a logarithmic measure related to hydrogen-ion activity. A lower pH usually indicates a more acidic solution, while a higher pH usually indicates a more basic solution. Neutrality depends on temperature and the solution, so pH 7 is the familiar neutral value near room temperature for pure water rather than a universal rule for every condition.

Corrosion is the gradual degradation of a material through chemical or electrochemical reactions with its environment. Iron can rust when oxygen and moisture participate in reactions that produce iron oxides and related compounds. Salt can accelerate corrosion by increasing the conductivity of moisture on a surface. Protective coatings, suitable alloys, design choices, and maintenance can reduce corrosion, although no method is perfect in every environment.

Thermal conductivity describes how readily a material transfers heat through conduction. Metals typically conduct heat more effectively than many plastics or foams. Insulation works by reducing heat transfer, often by trapping still air or using structures that impede conduction and convection. The best insulating material depends on temperature, moisture, compression, safety, durability, and cost.

Electrical conductivity describes how easily electric charge moves through a material. Copper and aluminum are widely used in electrical wiring because they conduct electricity well. Insulating materials such as rubber and many plastics resist current flow under ordinary conditions. Temperature, defects, moisture, voltage, and mechanical damage can alter a material's behavior.

Polymers can be natural, such as cellulose and natural rubber, or synthetic, such as polyethylene and nylon. Their properties depend on molecular structure, chain length, branching, crystallinity, additives, and processing. Some polymers soften when heated and can be reshaped; others form cross-linked networks that do not melt in the same way. Recycling is affected by polymer type, contamination, collection systems, and the economics of processing.

Life-cycle assessment evaluates environmental impacts associated with a product across stages such as raw-material extraction, manufacturing, transport, use, and disposal. A reusable product is not automatically better in every situation; the result depends on how often it is used, how it is cleaned, how it is produced, and what it replaces. Comparing alternatives requires consistent boundaries and assumptions.

Materials science connects microscopic structure to large-scale performance. Heat treatment can change the structure of metals, small amounts of an alloying element can alter strength or corrosion resistance, and arranging fibers in a composite can influence how loads are carried. Engineers test materials under conditions similar to their intended use because a material that performs well in one setting may fail in another.
