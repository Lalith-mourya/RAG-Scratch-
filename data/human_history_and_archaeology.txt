HUMAN HISTORY, ARCHAEOLOGY, AND MATERIAL EVIDENCE

History investigates human societies and change over time using written records, oral traditions, artifacts, buildings, images, and other evidence. Archaeology focuses on material remains of past human activity, including tools, pottery, bones, seeds, structures, and modified landscapes. The two fields often complement one another, but archaeology can study communities that left few or no written records.

An artifact is an object made, modified, or used by people. Stone tools can reveal manufacturing skills and the tasks for which they may have been used. Pottery can provide evidence about food preparation, storage, trade, and artistic styles. A single artifact rarely explains an entire society, so researchers interpret objects alongside their location, condition, and association with other finds.

Stratigraphy studies layers of soil, sediment, and deposits. In an undisturbed sequence, lower layers are generally older than layers above them. However, erosion, digging, burrowing animals, construction, and other disturbances can move objects between layers. Archaeologists record the position and context of each find because context often provides more information than the object alone.

Radiocarbon dating estimates the age of once-living material by measuring the remaining amount of radioactive carbon-14. Organisms take in carbon while alive, and the carbon-14 content changes after death at a known rate. The method is useful for materials such as wood, charcoal, seeds, and bone containing suitable carbon. It does not directly date most stone tools, and results require calibration and careful interpretation.

Dendrochronology uses patterns of tree rings to establish dates. In regions where trees produce distinct annual rings, researchers can compare ring-width patterns among living trees, historical timber, and archaeological wood. Tree rings can also preserve information about past growing conditions. The method depends on suitable wood and overlapping reference sequences.

Trade networks connect communities through the movement of goods, people, and ideas. Materials found far from their geological source can provide evidence of exchange. The distribution of particular stones, shells, metals, or ceramics may help researchers reconstruct contact between settlements. An object's presence at a site does not automatically reveal whether it arrived through direct trade, multiple exchanges, migration, or another process.

The development of agriculture occurred in multiple regions and at different times. Cultivating plants and managing animals changed food production and settlement patterns, but the transition was not a single event shared by all communities. Farming could support larger populations in some circumstances while also introducing new challenges, including labor demands, changing diets, disease exposure, and dependence on particular crops.

Ancient cities often depended on organized labor, water management, food storage, trade, and systems of governance. Streets, walls, drainage channels, temples, workshops, and houses can reveal how space was used. Urban layouts vary greatly, and researchers must avoid assuming that every city followed the same political or economic model.

Oral history records memories and traditions through interviews and community knowledge. It can preserve perspectives absent from official documents, although memory changes and stories may serve cultural or social purposes. Researchers compare oral accounts with other evidence while respecting the communities that maintain them.

Archaeological interpretation is provisional. New discoveries, improved dating techniques, and different research questions can change earlier conclusions. Responsible fieldwork documents locations carefully, follows legal requirements, consults descendant and local communities, and avoids removing objects without recording their context.
