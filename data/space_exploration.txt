SPACE EXPLORATION AND THE SOLAR SYSTEM

The Solar System consists of the Sun and all objects that orbit it, including eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids, and comets. The Sun contains most of the mass of the Solar System and provides the energy that supports life on Earth.

The eight planets in order from the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun, while Neptune is the farthest among the eight planets. Jupiter is the largest planet, and Mercury is the smallest.

Mars is often called the Red Planet because iron minerals in its soil oxidize and give its surface a reddish appearance. Scientists study Mars to understand whether microbial life could have existed there in the past. Mars has two small moons named Phobos and Deimos.

Saturn is famous for its extensive ring system, which consists mainly of ice particles, rocky debris, and dust. Although other giant planets also have rings, Saturn's rings are the most prominent when observed from Earth.

A light-year is a unit of distance rather than time. It represents the distance light travels through a vacuum in one year, approximately 9.46 trillion kilometers. Astronomers use light-years to describe distances between stars and galaxies.

Black holes are regions of space where gravity is so strong that nothing, including light, can escape from within the event horizon. Some black holes form when massive stars collapse at the end of their life cycles. Scientists can detect black holes by observing their gravitational effects on nearby matter and stars.

Space telescopes allow astronomers to observe celestial objects without many of the distortions caused by Earth's atmosphere. The James Webb Space Telescope primarily observes infrared light and helps researchers study early galaxies, star formation, and the atmospheres of distant planets.

The International Space Station is a habitable research laboratory orbiting Earth. Astronauts conduct experiments there involving biology, physics, materials science, and the effects of microgravity on the human body.

Space exploration continues to improve our understanding of the universe and contributes to technologies used in communication, navigation, weather forecasting, and Earth observation.
