FORESTS, PLANTS, AND ECOSYSTEM RELATIONSHIPS

Forests are ecosystems dominated by trees and other woody plants. They vary with climate, soil, elevation, and water availability. Tropical forests occur in warm regions with high rainfall, temperate forests experience seasonal changes, and boreal forests are found across cold northern regions.

Plants capture energy from sunlight through photosynthesis. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy that helps convert carbon dioxide and water into sugars, releasing oxygen as a by-product. The sugars provide energy and building material for growth. Photosynthesis connects plant life to many food webs because animals and decomposers depend directly or indirectly on plant-produced organic matter.

Roots anchor plants and absorb water and dissolved minerals from soil. Stems support leaves and transport water, minerals, and sugars through vascular tissues. Xylem mainly transports water and minerals from roots, while phloem distributes sugars and other organic compounds throughout the plant.

Leaves are adapted to collect light and exchange gases. Tiny pores called stomata allow carbon dioxide to enter and water vapor to leave. Guard cells regulate the opening of stomata. When water is scarce, many plants reduce stomatal opening to limit water loss, although this can also reduce carbon dioxide uptake and slow photosynthesis.

Soil is a mixture of mineral particles, organic matter, water, air, and living organisms. Soil texture depends on the proportions of sand, silt, and clay. Sandy soils drain quickly but often retain fewer nutrients, while clay-rich soils can hold water and nutrients but may drain slowly. Organic matter improves soil structure and supports organisms that recycle nutrients.

Mycorrhizal fungi form associations with the roots of many plant species. Fungal threads can extend through soil and help plants access water and minerals, especially phosphorus. In return, plants supply fungi with carbon compounds produced through photosynthesis. These relationships can influence plant growth and the composition of plant communities.

Forest ecosystems store carbon in trunks, branches, roots, leaves, and soil. When trees grow, they remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and incorporate carbon into biomass. Disturbances such as fire, drought, disease, and land clearing can release stored carbon or reduce future carbon uptake.

Biodiversity describes the variety of life at genetic, species, and ecosystem levels. A diverse forest may contain trees of different ages, shrubs, fungi, insects, birds, mammals, and microorganisms. Different species perform different roles, including pollination, seed dispersal, decomposition, and herbivore control.

Ecological succession is change in a community over time. After a disturbance that leaves soil intact, grasses and herbs may grow first, followed by shrubs and trees. After a new surface forms with little soil, such as bare rock, succession can begin more slowly as organisms help create suitable conditions. Succession does not always lead to one fixed endpoint because climate, disturbance, and species interactions continue to shape ecosystems.

Forest conservation can include protecting intact habitats, restoring degraded areas with suitable native species, maintaining wildlife corridors, and managing fire and invasive species. Planting trees can be useful, but the best approach depends on the original ecosystem. Planting trees in natural grasslands, for example, may damage habitats that already support specialized biodiversity.
